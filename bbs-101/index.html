BBS 101

What is a BBS?

A bulletin board system is a computer you log into. Other people log into the same computer. You leave messages, take files, play games, and sometimes talk live. That is the whole trick.

The first boards showed up at the end of the 1970s. Ward Christensen and Randy Suess built CBBS in Chicago during a blizzard. Through the 1980s and early 1990s, thousands of hobbyists ran boards out of closets and spare bedrooms, one or two phone lines at a time. You dialed a number. The modem screamed. A menu appeared in 16 colors, or in plain ASCII if you were unlucky.

The internet did not kill the idea so much as it outran the phone bill. Forums, email, and later the web did “leave a note for the next person” at global scale. Most boards went dark. A stubborn remainder never did. Others came back on telnet and SSH, which is how you reach The Boneyard today — same ritual, no long-distance charges.

It is not a website

You do not scroll a BBS in a browser tab (not the real one). You connect with a terminal client, you get a node, and you move through menus with keys. Time can be limited. What you type stays in a message base until someone answers — tomorrow, next week, or never. There is no algorithm trying to keep you agitated. There is a sysop, a few dozen regulars, and the weather of a small room.

The shape of a session

  1. Connect (telnet, SSH, or, on some boards, still a modem).
  2. Log in with a handle and a password.
  3. Read news, then mail, then public messages.
  4. Wander files, doors, and chat if the board has them.
  5. Log off. The machine keeps running without you.
